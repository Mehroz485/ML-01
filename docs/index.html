Content Refresh & Opportunity Scoring — FlyRank ML Internship Capstone

Which pages should you refresh first?

A content-decline priority model built on real search data — and the 15-point validation number that turned out not to be real.

Lane Refresh / Content Opportunity Scoring Data window March 2026 Scope 55 clients, 331,437 pages Honest precision@50 0.448
Abstract

Given a fixed weekly review capacity, which content pages should a team look at first, and with what action? Built on FlyRank's search warehouse — 9.8M daily performance rows across 331,437 pages and 55 clients for March 2026 — this model scores each page's risk of a within-month impression decline, using only the first half of the month's data. A 4-feature logistic regression, validated with a client-grouped split, reaches a precision@50 of 0.448 against a 0.291 base rate — modest, real, above-chance signal. A naive random row split on the identical data reported 0.596 at the same K: a result that does not survive once same-client leakage between train and test is removed, and the gap between those two numbers is the central finding of this paper. The output is a four-way ranked action playbook — priority refresh, content-only refresh, snippet cleanup, or monitor — intended for human review, not automation.

01 — Introduction

The decision this supports

A content team has more pages than time. Every week, someone has to decide which handful get a look — a rewrite, a refreshed title, or nothing — out of a warehouse holding hundreds of thousands.

Picking wrong is expensive in two directions. Reviewing a page that was already fine wastes a writer's afternoon. Ignoring a page that's genuinely sliding lets it keep sliding until it's expensive to recover. A short, ordered, explainable list — look at these first, and here's why — is worth more to that team than a dashboard of correlations.

This paper builds that list for one lane: Refresh / Content Opportunity Scoring. It scores pages by how likely they are to be entering a decline, cross-references that against a simpler, older signal (whether the page's click-through rate lags what similar-position pages typically get), and turns the result into a playbook — while being explicit, throughout, about how far the evidence actually reaches.

03 — Methodology

Label, features, baseline, validation

The label — a proxy, stated as one

is_declining = 1 if a page's impressions in the second half of March (days 16–31) fell more than 20% below its first half (days 1–15), else 0. Base rate: 29.1%. This is a within-month proxy for decline, not a validated ground truth — see Limitations.

Catching leakage before it could hide anywhere else

Before trusting any feature, the notebook deliberately built one that shouldn't be trusted: a column computed directly from the outcome window itself. Training on it alongside the honest features moved AUC from a real 0.597 to a suspicious 1.000 — the label, smuggled back in. The column was removed and the honest number kept. That test is the standard every feature below had to clear.

Features — four, all pre-decision

Four signals, each summed or averaged over days 1–15 of March only, so nothing used to score a page has seen the days its label is built from: imp_early, clk_early, ctr_early, pos_early.

A fifth feature, days_active_early, was tested and cut: permutation importance came back negative, and a direct with/without check confirmed it — held-out AUC rose from 0.472 to 0.544 once it was removed. Carrying a feature that measurably hurts into a production queue didn't survive being written down.

Baseline — a hand-written rule, checked before trusted

A page is flagged if it has real volume, ranks well enough to be seen, and under-performs its position's typical click-through rate. Both assumptions behind that rule were tested, not assumed:

The second assumption — a 500-impression floor before trusting a page's CTR — also held: below that floor, CTR standard deviation is 0.0495; above it, roughly 0.003, a ~16× difference in noise.

Validation — grouped, then audited again

Random row splits were rejected on principle: with 41 clients averaging ~2,700 rows each, a random split all but guarantees the same client's other pages sit in both train and test. GroupKFold on client_hash_id was used instead — until auditing the split itself found a problem: one fold's test set landed on a single client, and base rates swung from 0.198 to 0.394 fold to fold. Switching to StratifiedGroupKFold kept the client grouping but balanced label rates across folds, cutting the fold-to-fold standard deviation at precision@50 from 0.208 to 0.138.

Three methods were compared, in order of complexity: Logistic Regression, a depth-3 Decision Tree (shallow enough to read end to end), and Random Forest — the standard progression for a yes/no observed label evaluated as a ranking problem.

04 — Results

The honest number, and the one that didn't survive

Same data, same model, same K — three validation designs, three very different answers.

base 29.1%
GroupKFold by client first honest attempt0.440 ± 0.208
StratifiedGroupKFold final, reported number0.448 ± 0.138

Precision@50, mean ± std across 5 grouped folds. Grey line = base rate (29.1%) — what you'd get flagging pages at random. Note how much tighter the naive split's std looks — that's part of what makes it deceptively convincing.

Why the naive number is wrong, not just different. A plain 80/20 random split on the same rows and the same model reports 0.596 — a fifteen-point improvement over the honest number, and a tighter-looking std (0.038 vs 0.138). Both are artifacts of the same cause: with only 41 clients, a random split leaks each client's other pages across train and test, letting the model partly memorize client-level baselines instead of learning transferable signal. The grouped number is the real one.

Does the model beat the honest baseline?

Directionally, yes — Logistic Regression reaches 0.440 at precision@50 under GroupKFold vs. the early-window CTR-gap rule's 0.420, and the pattern holds at precision@200 (0.444 vs. 0.342). But the 0.02 gap at K=50 sits well inside a 0.117–0.208 fold-to-fold standard deviation, given only 41 clients. This is decision-support evidence, not a confirmed win — worded exactly that carefully because the evidence doesn't stretch further.

What the model gets wrong

Permutation importance ranks ctr_early, pos_early, and imp_early as carrying real signal (0.042–0.048); clk_early is weaker (0.019). Errors concentrate in page_1 positions (30.8% error rate) — the mid-pack zone where a page could plausibly tip either way. False negatives tend to look stable across the 15-day window and decline anyway — nothing in a snapshot that short catches a late-month event. False positives tend to be low-CTR, deep-position pages that simply recovered on their own.

05 — Limitations & honest framing

What this cannot claim

  • The model's edge over the baseline is directional, not confirmed. 0.448 vs. 0.420 at precision@50 sits inside the fold-to-fold noise from a 41-client sample.
  • The label is a within-month proxy, not ground truth. A real impression decline could be seasonal, a client-side site change, or a shifting SERP feature — none of which "refresh the content" fixes. This model flags a pattern, not a diagnosis.
  • One month, one warehouse release, ~41–55 clients. No claim this generalizes to a different season, a different client mix, or a different search vertical without re-validation.
  • Click data is sparse. Median CTR is exactly 0 in most position buckets — the aggregate CTR-cliff pattern held, but any single page's CTR-gap flag is noisy.
  • This is decision-support for a human review queue — not an automated action system, and not a claim about how any search engine's ranking algorithm works.

Required human checks before acting

  1. Confirm the decline isn't explained by a known client-side event (template change, expired product, deliberate pivot) before recommending content work.
  2. For refresh/snippet archetypes, check whether a SERP feature is now absorbing clicks at that position before assuming the title or meta is the problem.
  3. Treat rows near the 500-impression floor with extra skepticism — CTR is noisiest right around that threshold.

Should not be automated

  • ✕ Auto-publishing rewritten content, titles, or meta descriptions without a human edit pass.
  • ✕ Auto-archiving or deprioritizing "monitor only" pages — absence of this signal isn't proof of health.
  • ✕ Using the decline-probability score as a client-facing health score or contractual SLA metric.
  • ✕ Treating this list as exhaustive site-wide prioritization — it covers one lane, one month, one client cohort.
